## What an accurate valuation actually means for a broken Jaguar

Type a registration and mileage into a well known valuation site and you expect a number back. What you actually get is a figure built for a car that starts on the first turn of the key, drives smoothly onto a forecourt, and can be looked over by someone standing next to it. Every tool ranking for this kind of search shares that same assumption, whether it says so or not.

That's the real reason two different valuation sites can return two very different numbers for the same registration plate. It isn't that one tool is more accurate than the other. Neither was built to price a Jaguar that won't start, has been in an accident, or hasn't seen an MOT tester in years.

If your Jaguar does start, drives properly, holds a valid MOT and V5C, and is in generally sound condition, none of this applies to you. It's a normal used car, and a mainstream valuation site, a dealer part-exchange or a private sale will likely get you a fairer figure, because those routes are pricing a healthy car against the wider retail market. What follows is for the car those routes can't or won't price properly: something with a fault, missing paperwork, or a condition an ordinary appraisal can't get near.

## Why the reg-and-mileage tools stop working once the car doesn't run

An instant valuation calculator works from a condition grade, usually inferred from the car's age and mileage, with roadworthiness assumed until someone checks it in person. That grade then gets adjusted, upward or downward, once an inspector actually sees the car. It's a system built around a car that can be started, driven, and looked over.

Take away the ability to start or drive the car and that whole process has nothing to grade. The tool either refuses to produce a figure at all, or it produces one that quietly treats the car as scrap without saying so. Neither outcome tells you what your Jaguar is actually worth.

So when people ask which valuation tool is "most accurate" for a car like this, the honest answer isn't about which brand to trust. It's about whether the tool was built to price the condition your car is actually in. A Jaguar that won't start needs a valuation built around exactly that question, rather than a calculator designed for one that merely drives badly. ## The paperwork most calculators quietly assume you still have

Look closely at how the mainstream tools ask for information and you'll notice they assume a V5C and a current MOT already sit behind the registration number you've typed in. That's the paperwork trail an ordinary used-car valuation depends on, because it's the paperwork trail an ordinary used car actually has.

A valuation aimed at a broken Jaguar doesn't need any of that. A V5 logbook isn't required, and neither is an MOT or a car that can be driven. That single difference is usually the reason someone whose Jaguar has been sitting untaxed on a driveway for years, or was recorded as scrapped on some system long ago, gets nowhere typing a registration into a mainstream site.

If you genuinely have no V5C at all, or you're not sure what to do about notifying DVLA, that's worth reading properly on its own rather than skimming a paragraph here. See [DVLA paperwork when you sell a broken car](/blog/dvla-paperwork-selling-broken-car) for the full picture.

## Priced as a car, not weighed in as scrap

A scrap or breaker valuation prices a car by weight and by what can be stripped from it for parts. A specialist valuation prices the whole car, faults included, because a Jaguar with a known problem is still a largely intact vehicle rather than a pile of metal. Something like an Ingenium timing chain rattle, a ZF gearbox stuck in limp mode, or air suspension that's collapsed on one corner is a repair, not a reason to send the car to a crusher. Our [common Jaguar faults guide](/blog/common-jaguar-faults) walks through the failures that scare general buyers but are everyday business for a specialist.

That distinction is why specialist pricing can beat scrap-weight pricing, sometimes by a wide margin. It's also the reason it's worth getting more than one figure before accepting the first number offered, particularly if that first figure came from a scrap yard or a breaker rather than someone who buys and understands Jaguars specifically.

## Why Jaguars can look cheap on the general valuation sites right now

If a Jaguar's figure on a generic valuation tool looks low next to an equivalent German saloon, there's nothing mysterious behind it. It comes down to a handful of known, sometimes expensive faults, an Ingenium timing chain that wears, a ZF gearbox that can fail once it's out of warranty, air suspension and its compressor giving out, feeding into how cautious the wider used market has become about the marque once a car is past its warranty years.

That's a statement about the general market's caution, not about your specific car. A Jaguar with one of those faults already diagnosed and understood is a different, more answerable question than the average figure a generic tool is drawing on. It has a known cause and a known fix, rather than being an unknown risk a buyer has to price in.

## Where the hidden fees usually sit, and where they don't

Where fee transparency comes up at all on competitor sites, it's almost always about admin fees on a standard sale or the small print on a part-exchange deal. Almost none of them address what it actually costs to get a car that can't be driven from a driveway to a buyer, which for a broken Jaguar is the fee question that actually matters.

The real question isn't what the valuation costs, it's what gets taken off between the offer being made and the money landing in your account. Is collection charged for? Is a trailer treated as an extra? Is anything deducted on the day once the car's actually in front of someone? Collection is free anywhere in mainland UK, at no cost to the seller, which is the direct answer to that specific worry rather than a general selling point.

## Write-offs: a valuation question insurers and calculators both skip

A Jaguar already carrying a Cat S or Cat N marker is a different valuation question again. Most mainstream tools either won't produce a figure once that marker shows up, or they quietly assume the car is worth very little because of the marker itself, rather than looking at the condition of the car underneath it.

Salvage-category Jaguars, across the recognised categories, are bought here and priced on the car rather than on the category label alone. If your Jaguar already carries a Cat S or Cat N marker, you'll likely already know what that means for the car; what matters for the valuation is what's actually wrong with it, not just which letter is attached to the record.

## What actually makes a Jaguar valuation trustworthy

Whichever tool you're using, and whichever one you use next, the same handful of questions tell you whether it was built for a car like yours. Does it have a route for a car that doesn't run, rather than assuming one? Does it ask for a V5C or a current MOT before it will give you any figure at all? Does it say anything about what gets deducted between the offer and the payment landing? And does it explain why the figure moves, rather than quoting the same number for every car regardless of condition?

On that last point: prices actually paid for a broken Jaguar through this business have run from £1,200 to £10,000, and that range only means anything alongside what moves a car within it, the model, the mileage, the specification and which fault it's carrying. A number with nothing attached just sets an expectation that collection day then has to correct, which helps nobody.
